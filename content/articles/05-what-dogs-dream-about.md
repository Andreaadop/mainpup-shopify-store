# What Your Dog Is Dreaming About (Based on Science and Suspicion)

Your dog is asleep. Their paws are moving. Their lips are pulled back in a small, quiet snarl. A muffled *woof* escapes them. Somewhere behind those closed eyelids, something is *happening*.

You want to know what.

The good news: science has more answers than you'd think. The better news: for the questions science can't answer, we've developed theories.

## First, the science: yes, dogs really do dream

This isn't in doubt anymore. Research at MIT and elsewhere in the 2000s confirmed that dogs experience REM sleep — the same rapid-eye-movement phase of sleep in which humans have most of our vivid, story-based dreams.

Here's what's confirmed:

- **Dogs enter REM sleep** roughly 20 minutes after they fall asleep and cycle in and out of it throughout their nap or nightly rest.
- **Brain activity in a sleeping dog** looks structurally similar to a sleeping human — including in the regions responsible for memory consolidation and emotional processing.
- **Small dogs dream more often** — as often as every 10 minutes. Large dogs dream less frequently but for longer stretches.
- **Puppies and elderly dogs dream the most** — probably because both are consolidating a lot of new information (puppies) or reprocessing accumulated memories (seniors).

So when your dog is twitching, running in their sleep, or making little muffled woofs — that's a real dream. They are, in some meaningful sense, *experiencing* something.

---

## What they're probably dreaming about (per the research)

Neuroscientists have made a good guess based on what dogs' brains do while awake vs. asleep. The current best theory is that dogs dream about **the specific activities they do most often when awake.**

Which means, statistically:

**They're dreaming about you.** The person they see most, spend the most time with, and are most emotionally attached to almost certainly appears in their dreams. Probably a lot.

**They're dreaming about walks.** Especially recently novel walks. New smells, new places, new routes.

**They're dreaming about play.** Chase, tug-of-war, retrieving. If they play with a specific person or another specific dog, that individual is likely showing up.

**They're dreaming about food.** Come on.

**They're dreaming about the routine.** Repeatedly experienced events (the morning walk, dinner time, greeting you at the door) get processed and reinforced during sleep. Your dog dreams the story of their own life.

## What we suspect they're dreaming about (based on observation)

The scientific answers above are almost certainly true. What follows is a working theory.

### The chase they didn't finish

Every dog has an unresolved chase in their memory. A squirrel they didn't catch. A rabbit that made it into the hedge. A frisbee that landed on the roof. In REM sleep, we suspect they're finishing it.

This would explain the running motions. It would also explain the small satisfied sigh at the end.

### The specific person they miss

If you've ever had a dog whose beloved human moved away or passed on, you've seen them wake up sometimes and look, briefly, for that person. We suspect the dream included them.

This is not a scientifically verified claim. It is, however, an experientially verified one.

### Past lives (in the mainpups sense)

If your dog was, in a former existence, a Chef — we suspect the dreams involve the kitchen they lost. If they were the Retired Spy, we suspect the dreams involve missions we don't have clearance for. If they were the Lounge Singer, we suspect the dreams involve a specific stage in Las Vegas circa 1972.

We cannot prove this. But you know what your dog looks like when they dream about food (excited paws). And you know what they look like when they dream about something else, something specific, something you don't recognize. That's the past life dream. That's the one we made a [quiz](/quiz/) for.

## Should you wake them up?

Generally no. Even if the dream looks distressing.

Two reasons:

1. **REM sleep is important.** It's when memory consolidates. Interrupting it means your dog is processing less effectively.
2. **A dog woken from a bad dream can startle badly.** Even the sweetest dog can nip in the confused half-second after being jolted out of a nightmare. Not because they're mean — because they're briefly unaware where they are.

If a dream really seems distressing, the gentler move is to softly say their name from a safe distance — let them wake themselves up. Then, once they're awake, sit with them for a minute. They don't need anything specific. They just need to check that you're still there.

---

## What to make of it

Your dog has an inner life. That inner life includes dreams. Those dreams are shaped by their memories, their experiences, their preferences, and — we strongly suspect — some things they haven't fully told you about.

The next time you catch your dog mid-dream, watch for a second before you move to comfort them. What they're doing in there is important. It's also, in a small quiet way, evidence that they are exactly as complex as you always suspected.
